# POS cashier and kitchen (KDS) action volume, throughput, and efficiency

- Date: 2026-09-05
- Question: In real-world operation, how many discrete actions does a POS cashier and a kitchen (KDS) user do, and what do throughput and efficiency data say? This informs Umi, a coffee-shop/QSR POS + Kitchen Display System.
- Scope: actions per transaction (taps/clicks/steps), transactions per cashier per hour/shift, KDS interactions per ticket and per shift, and the human-factors and operations research that links tap-count and screen flow to speed and error rate. Coffee shop and QSR context, counter and drive-thru.
- Method: primary and high-trust sources only — peer-reviewed HCI/operations papers, first-party vendor help docs and engineering/press pages, company earnings calls and newsrooms, and the industry drive-thru study that owns the timing data. Each figure carries its owning-source link and a label: MEASURED (observed study), CLAIM (company self-report), or ESTIMATE (secondary, analyst, or a count derived by reading a flow). Figures are the values observed on 2026-09-05.

## 1. Summary

No public primary source states a single canonical "taps per POS order" or "taps per kitchen ticket" number. That figure is not published; it must be derived. Two methods are defensible, and this report uses both: (a) count the steps in the vendor's own documented UI flow, and (b) apply the Keystroke-Level Model (KLM), a validated HCI model that predicts expert task time from a count of low-level operators. Both are ESTIMATES, not measurements. The report labels them as such.

This report answers "how many actions" along two different dimensions, and they are not the same size:

- **Software actions (screen taps)** — the taps and clicks a cashier or KDS user makes on the POS/KDS device. Covered in Sections 4-6.
- **Physical actions (bodily steps/motions)** — the bodily steps and hand motions the human worker makes to serve the customer and craft the product. Covered in Section 3.

The physical budget dwarfs the software one. A single espresso milk drink is ~20 discrete physical motions (derived below), while the KDS ticket that represents it costs ~2-4 taps. So the POS/KDS screen is a small part of the worker's real action load. The design goal is to add no avoidable software friction on top of an already motion-heavy job, and to remove non-value physical motion where the leading chains have (sequencing, routing, mise en place).

What is measured and primary is the surrounding operations data: drive-thru service times, mobile-order adoption, and the throughput initiatives that the largest chains describe in their own earnings calls and newsrooms. These set the benchmark that a POS/KDS action model should serve.

Best-sourced findings:

1. **The action count is a design lever, not a vendor statistic.** The KLM (Card, Moran & Newell, 1980; Kieras, 1997) assigns ~0.2 s to a keystroke/button press and ~1.35 s to a mental-preparation step, and predicts task time within ~20-21% of observed time — so a POS that removes 2-3 taps and one decision point per checkout removes real, measurable seconds, multiplied by every transaction ([Kieras KLM](https://www.cs.umd.edu/~golbeck/INST631/KSM.pdf), [ACM/CACM original](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/358886.358895)).
2. **Vendor checkout flows are short by design.** Square's documented flow is roughly "tap an item tile → tap Charge → tip screen → card tap → done"; a bare one-item sale is ~3 taps, and each customized item adds ~3 (variation + modifiers + Add to cart). These are ESTIMATES derived from Square's own help docs ([build cart](https://squareup.com/help/us/en/article/8238-build-your-customer-s-cart-in-the-square-retail-pos-app), [tipping/flow](https://squareup.com/help/us/en/article/8631-set-up-and-customize-tipping)).
3. **KDS bump is ~1-2 actions per ticket in shipping products.** Square KDS = 1 tap to complete a whole ticket (3-second undo); Toast KDS = a double-tap or two bump-bar presses; Lightspeed KDS 2.0 = a double-tap per state change (5-second undo). All ESTIMATES read from first-party docs ([Square KDS](https://squareup.com/help/us/en/article/8171-complete-orders-with-square-kds), [Toast KDS](https://doc.toasttab.com/doc/platformguide/platformKDSOverview.html), [Lightspeed KDS 2.0](https://k-series-support.lightspeedhq.com/hc/en-us/articles/22708154090267-Using-the-Kitchen-Display-System-2-0)).
4. **Coffee-shop throughput is framed in minutes-to-handoff, not transactions-per-hour.** Starbucks' public target is to hand the order to the customer in ≤4 minutes; the CEO said ~50% of transactions already met that on his first earnings call (Q4 FY2024). Starbucks also says an order-sequencing algorithm "prioritized more than 100 million café orders and reduced production time by two minutes" (Investor Day, Jan 2026). Both are company CLAIMS ([4-minute goal](https://www.euronews.com/2024/10/31/starbucks-ceo-sets-four-minute-service-goal-to-reinvigorate-sales), [Investor Day](https://finance.yahoo.com/news/starbucks-investor-day-back-starbucks-140638312.html)).
5. **Drive-thru is the one place with hard, measured, third-party seconds.** The 2025 QSR Drive-Thru Study (owned by Intouch Insight) measured a classic-segment average total time of 5 min 9 s, average service time of 4 min 15 s, and 87% order accuracy across ~13 brands ([Intouch 2025 release](https://www.intouchinsight.com/press-releases/drive-thru-study-2025-press-release)). MEASURED.
6. **The second/digital make line is the real-world KDS-driven kitchen case.** Chipotle has run a "second make line" for digital orders since 2018 (SEC 8-K) and now reports peak throughput of "low to mid-30s" entrees per 15 minutes, with a proprietary crew display ("Chipotle Kitchen") on the digital line (Q2 FY2026 call). Company CLAIMS ([2018 8-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0001058090/000119312518206319/d656360dex991.htm), [Q2 2026 transcript](https://www.fool.com/earnings/call-transcripts/2026/08/07/chipotle-cmg-q2-2026-earnings-call-transcript/)).
7. **Weak-evidence flag.** The popular figures "kiosks lift the average check ~20-30%," "KDS cuts errors ~90% / wait ~20-30%," and "McDonald's kiosks add ~$1 / 30% per order" have no confirmed first-party owner; they trace to vendor blogs and analysts, not to a company release or a peer-reviewed study. One peer-reviewed field experiment (a Burger King study) found the opposite direction on sales: kiosks were faster but produced _lower_ sales per customer ([UNT thesis](https://digital.library.unt.edu/ark:/67531/metadc1505242/)). Treat the uplift numbers as ESTIMATE/secondary.

Best-sourced physical-action findings:

8. **An espresso milk drink is ~20 discrete physical motions.** The espresso shot alone is a documented 14-step routine, and ~6 of those are the core hands-on actions (grind → dose → distribute → tamp → lock → extract). These counts are ESTIMATES derived from a published craft routine, not a certified figure ([Perfect Daily Grind, 14 steps](https://perfectdailygrind.com/2018/01/barista-basics-how-to-make-an-espresso-in-14-steps/), [SCA Coffee Skills Program](https://education.sca.coffee/)).
9. **Barista work is measurably injurious, and tamping is the worst step.** A peer-reviewed study of 59 baristas (10 analyzed on video) found 73% reported low-back pain and 68% shoulder pain, with lumbar compression during tamping of ~2,800-4,100 N against the NIOSH 3,400 N action limit (Dainty et al., 2014, _Ergonomics_) ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24837283/)). MEASURED.
10. **A QSR line splits one ticket across bodies and many motions.** McDonald's runs the line as Initiator → Assembler → Presenter across ~18 station checklists; a single built item is ~10-20 discrete hand actions (ESTIMATE), and food-safety rules add a handwash/glove-change action set at every raw→ready-to-eat task change (FDA Food Code 2022) ([McDonald's SOC index](https://sites.google.com/view/mcdsoc), [FDA Food Code](https://www.fda.gov/media/164194/download)).
11. **The chains compete on removing physical motion, not screen taps.** Chipotle's throughput doctrine is dedicated expo + "linebacker" roles that keep crew hands on the line (linebacker deployed in 70%+ of restaurants, Q2 FY2026), and time-and-motion redesigns cut kitchen-staff travel ~40% and lifted service speed 10-20% (Pollo Campero) ([Chipotle transcript](https://www.fool.com/earnings/call-transcripts/2026/08/07/chipotle-cmg-q2-2026-earnings-call-transcript/), [Foodservice Equipment Reports](https://www.fermag.com/articles/6282-its-about-time-and-motion/)). CLAIM.
12. **Work-measurement gives the unit and the injury threshold.** MTM sets 1 TMU = 0.036 s and decomposes every "action" into micro-motions (a simple pick-up ≈ 2 TMU); the >10 upper-limb reps/minute injury threshold (Texas DWC / OSHA) is crossed at a busy bar or line, which is why the ergonomics studies find high strain ([MTM/Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Methods-time_measurement), [Texas DWC](https://www.tdi.texas.gov/pubs/videoresource/fsergofood.pdf)). Standard / MEASURED.

## 3. Physical / real-life operational actions (bodily steps and motions)

This section counts the physical actions the human worker performs — the steps, reaches, and hand motions to serve a customer and craft the product. This is a different dimension from the software taps in Sections 4-6, and a much larger one. As with taps, no primary source publishes a canonical "motions per drink" or "motions per ticket." The counts below are derived by counting the steps in a documented craft or station routine (ESTIMATE), and are anchored to two solid foundations: predetermined-motion-time systems (MTM/MOST), which are standard-defined, and peer-reviewed barista/cashier ergonomics, which are MEASURED.

### 3.1 The measurement frame: work-measurement and service blueprinting

- **Predetermined motion time systems (MTM, MOST).** Methods-Time Measurement decomposes any manual task into basic micro-motions — Reach, Move, Turn, Apply Pressure, Grasp, Release, Position, Disengage — each with a standard time. The unit is the TMU: **1 TMU = 0.036 s, and 1 hour = 100,000 TMU** (so 1 s ≈ 27.78 TMU) ([Wikipedia MTM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Methods-time_measurement), [LIUC course notes](https://my.liuc.it/MatSup/2018/N91328/TIME%20AND%20METHOD%20II.pdf)). A concrete anchor: a **simple pick-up ≈ 2 TMU** in MTM-1, and "bend and arise" ≈ 61 TMU ([Predetermined motion time system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Predetermined_motion_time_system)). MOST rolls these into sequence models (General Move = A B G A B P A); a worked example — walk three steps, pick a bolt off the floor — totals 170 TMU ≈ 0.102 min ([LIUC](https://my.liuc.it/MatSup/2018/N91328/TIME%20AND%20METHOD%20II.pdf)). The lesson for Umi: every "action" a worker takes is itself several micro-motions with a real, standard time, so counting discrete actions understates the true motion load.
- **No MTM/MOST study of a specific espresso or QSR-assembly task with published standard seconds was found in a primary source.** The frame is validated; a foodservice-specific standard-time table is not public.
- **Service blueprinting (Shostack, 1984, _HBR_).** The method maps a service into steps split by a "line of visibility" (customer-visible vs back-stage), marks fail points, and sets a standard execution time. Shostack's worked "Corner Shoeshine" blueprint inventories the steps (take shoes → apply wax → buff → collect payment), sets a **standard execution time of 2 minutes** against a **customer tolerance of 5 minutes**, and its fail point is "wrong color wax"; a modified version adds a second coat and a receipt, raising the standard to 3 minutes ([HBR](https://hbr.org/1984/01/designing-services-that-deliver); origin: Shostack 1982, _European Journal of Marketing_ [Emerald](https://www.emerald.com/ejm/article/16/1/49/39237/How-to-Design-a-Service)). This is the primary basis for treating counter service as a countable, timed step sequence.

### 3.2 Front of house — cashier/barista service sequence per customer

- **The service step sequence.** The customer-facing sequence — greet → take order → enter it → suggestive-sell → take payment → give change and receipt → call the name / hand off — is roughly **7-8 discrete steps per customer**. This exact QSR-counter model is **not published verbatim in a primary/academic source** (it lives in operations and training material); the nearest primary anchors are Shostack's blueprint method (3.1) and the restaurant application below. Treat the 7-8 count as an ESTIMATE.
- **The one measured counter time-and-motion study.** Hummel & Murphy (2011, _Cornell Hospitality Quarterly_) ran a **time-and-motion study of 152 transactions at six restaurants** of a full-service casual chain, using a service blueprint. Findings relevant here: the suggestive-sell step (wine presentation) underperformed its sales expectation, and servers delayed check presentation against policy, raising table-occupancy time ([SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1938965511410687)). MEASURED. It confirms the step inventory (greet → suggestive-sell → order → check-back → payment → hand-off) and that the suggestive-sell and payment steps are where time and money leak.
- **Counter/cashier work is highly repetitive and measurably injurious.** Peer-reviewed cashier ergonomics consistently find high work-related musculoskeletal disorder (WMSD) prevalence in the neck, shoulders, wrists/hands, and back, driven by repetition and static posture (Roxô et al., 2021, _Rev. Bras. Med. Trab._ [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9137857/); RCT in _Int. Arch. Occup. Environ. Health_, 2025 [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00420-025-02132-z); the classic Marras-era characterization of supermarket checking, _Ergonomics_ 1992 [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1593292/)). MEASURED (prevalence and biomechanics). A specific "reaches per hour" count was **not extractable from an accessible primary abstract**.

### 3.3 Front of house — espresso drink motion inventory per drink

- **Espresso shot: ~14 documented steps, ~6 core hands-on.** A published craft routine lists 14 discrete steps to pull one shot: rinse cup, ready portafilter/basket, grind and dose, distribute, tamp, polish, clean excess, purge group head, insert and pull, serve at ~25 s, knock out puck, clean handle, rinse/brush group, replace portafilter ([Perfect Daily Grind](https://perfectdailygrind.com/2018/01/barista-basics-how-to-make-an-espresso-in-14-steps/)). The core hands-on subset — grind → dose → distribute → tamp → lock → extract — is ~6 actions. The 14 count is the article's own count of its routine (ESTIMATE).
- **A milk drink is ~20-22 discrete physical actions.** On top of the shot, a cappuccino/latte adds: purge steam wand → steam and texture milk (inject air, then roll) → wipe/purge wand → swirl jug → pour / latte art → (lid, sleeve, label for to-go). Combined with the shot routine this is roughly **~20-22 discrete physical actions per milk drink** (ESTIMATE, author's count of the quoted routines, cross-checked against the SCA Barista Skills routine — milk textured to 55-65 °C) ([PDG](https://perfectdailygrind.com/2018/01/barista-basics-how-to-make-an-espresso-in-14-steps/), [SCA Coffee Skills Program](https://education.sca.coffee/)). A multi-drink order scales roughly linearly: the per-drink grind/tamp/extract and milk/finish actions repeat, and only shared purges amortize.
- **Tamping is a forceful, repeated, high-risk motion.** The craft convention is to tamp with about **30 lb of force (~133 N)** ([PDG](https://perfectdailygrind.com/2018/01/barista-basics-how-to-make-an-espresso-in-14-steps/)) — CLAIM. Measured biomechanics back the risk: Dainty et al. (2014, _Ergonomics_) surveyed 59 baristas and analyzed 10 on video; **73% reported low-back pain and 68% shoulder pain**, tamping was the highest-risk bar task, and lumbar compression during tamping ran **~2,800-4,100 N against the NIOSH 3,400 N action limit** ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24837283/); Newton range via [Sprudge](https://sprudge.com/barista-injury-study-ergonomics-dainty-gregory-60680.html)). A follow-up (Gregory et al., 2017, _Int. J. Ind. Ergonomics_) put 10 baristas through 20 tamps each on a force plate and showed a redesigned tamper lowered applied force, RULA, and Strain Index scores ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0169814117301324)). MEASURED.
- **Per-drink build time.** The espresso extraction sub-step is ~25-30 s ([Clive Coffee](https://clivecoffee.com/blogs/faqs/espresso-extraction-time-ratio-a-complete-guide-clive-coffee)) — a craft standard (CLAIM). An industry cadence benchmark is "two minutes for four milk drinks," i.e. ~one cappuccino every ~30 s, with a modern assessment target of under 210 s for four drinks ([Barista Hustle](https://www.baristahustle.com/i-got-210-seconds-to-spro/)) — CLAIM. A measured, instrumented total seconds-to-make-a-latte figure was **not found in a primary/peer-reviewed source**.

### 3.4 Front of house — per-shift physical totals implied

- **Peak output per bar.** Café-equipment sources put a 2-group machine with two baristas at **~60-80 milk drinks/hour**, with bursts of 100+, and note the bottleneck is milk steaming and pouring, not the brew group ([Prima Coffee](https://prima-coffee.com/blog/the-cafe-equipment-buying-guide-espresso-machines/), [Visions Espresso](https://visionsespresso.com/blog/best-espresso-machine-for-coffee-shop/)). Industry/secondary, not primary.
- **Motion load for a Umi-scale bar.** At the Umi baseline of ~100-250 tickets/shift and ~20 physical actions per milk drink, a barista performs on the order of **~2,000-5,000+ discrete physical motions per shift** (ESTIMATE, author's arithmetic on the derived per-drink count). At a peak of 60-80 drinks/hour (~1-1.3 drinks/min × ~20 motions), the hands run at **~20-27 upper-limb motions per minute** — well above the **>10 reps/min** upper-limb injury threshold published by the Texas Division of Workers' Compensation and OSHA ([Texas DWC](https://www.tdi.texas.gov/pubs/videoresource/fsergofood.pdf), [OSHA food-services MSD eTool](https://www.osha.gov/etools/hospitals/food-services/work-related-musculoskeletal-disorders)). This is the mechanism behind the measured WMSD prevalence in 3.3.

### 3.5 Back of house — QSR line / assembly / expo per ticket

- **The per-ticket sequence.** Receive/read ticket → gather components → cook or assemble → plate/bag → bump → expo quality check → hand to runner is ~7 stages. A built item's "assemble/dress" stage alone is ~5-8 topping/condiment placements, so a single-item ticket is roughly **~10-20 discrete hand actions** (ESTIMATE — no primary source states a per-ticket count).
- **The line is split across bodies, not done by one worker.** McDonald's operationalizes the line as ~18 station checklists and explicitly splits a ticket across sequential roles — **Initiator → Assembler → Presenter** ([McDonald's SOC index](https://sites.google.com/view/mcdsoc); a single station such as fries decomposes into ~4-6 documented actions, [SOC example](https://forms-library.signnow.com/230768-mcdonalds-soc-pdf)). This is a leaked/unofficial SOC index, so treat it as secondary, not an official McDonald's publication.
- **Food-safety rules add physical actions.** FDA Food Code 2022 requires a handwash "when changing tasks" and "when switching between working with raw food and ready-to-eat food" (§2-301.14), a fresh single-use glove per task (a violation to reuse across tasks), and no bare-hand contact with ready-to-eat food (§3-301.11) ([FDA Food Code](https://www.fda.gov/media/164194/download)). Each raw→ready-to-eat changeover therefore adds a wash-and-re-glove action set — motions the ticket count never shows. The Code ties these to task/event changes, not a fixed clock interval.
- **Repetition on the line.** The same >10 reps/min (arms/wrists) and >2.5 reps/min (shoulders) injury thresholds apply to "slicing, dicing, flipping" food-prep motion ([Texas DWC](https://www.tdi.texas.gov/pubs/videoresource/fsergofood.pdf)); a patented griddle scraper is described as moved "as often as 50 times per hour" ([US Patent 6,263,578](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/6263578)), one small illustration of per-hour line motion.

### 3.6 Back of house — Chipotle line dynamics and per-shift throughput

- **The "four pillars of throughput."** Chipotle's doctrine is a dedicated **expo** (bags orders, gets drinks/sides, takes payment at the end of the line), a dedicated **linebacker** (restocks the line so servers never leave their stations), **mise en place** (prep so food is ready in seconds), and **"aces in their places"** (best crew at each position during peaks) ([origin coverage, Quartz](https://qz.com/172900/how-chipotle-is-going-to-serve-burritos-faster-and-faster-and-faster)). On the Q2 FY2026 call, the CEO called expo and linebacker "the 2 most important pillars," reported **linebacker deployment in over 70% of restaurants** for the first time, and quantified peak throughput as **"low to mid-30s" entrees in the peak 15 minutes ("Max 15")**, with the high-efficiency equipment package (HEEP) adding 2-3 entrees and installed in 1,000+ restaurants (targeting ~2,000) ([Chipotle transcript](https://www.fool.com/earnings/call-transcripts/2026/08/07/chipotle-cmg-q2-2026-earnings-call-transcript/)). CLAIM.
- **Per-hour assembly motion.** "Low-to-mid-30s" entrees per 15 minutes is ~120-140 entrees/hour at peak per restaurant. Each entrée is several discrete assembly actions (portion protein → rice → beans → salsas → toppings → fold/close → bag), so a make-line station runs into the several-hundred-motions-per-peak-hour range (ESTIMATE, author's scaling).
- **Chains compete by cutting physical motion.** Time-and-motion is an explicit QSR practice: a Wendy's industrial engineer measures "how many seconds we take to handle a piece of lettuce"; Smashburger cut cook times by a third; Snooze got a 30% reduction in ticket times; Pollo Campero's prototype eliminated ~40% of kitchen-staff travel distance and raised service speed 10-20% ([Foodservice Equipment Reports](https://www.fermag.com/articles/6282-its-about-time-and-motion/)). CLAIM (trade / chain-reported). The pattern: the throughput lever is fewer, shorter physical motions and less travel — not a faster screen.

### 3.7 Labor-productivity anchors

- **Labor cost.** Restaurant labor runs a median **31.7% of sales in limited-service** and 36.5% in full-service (National Restaurant Association, 2024 data) ([NRA](https://restaurant.org/research-and-media/research/restaurant-economic-insights/analysis-commentary/elevated-labor-costs-had-a-significant-impact-on-restaurant-profitability-in-2024/)). MEASURED (survey). Real restaurant sales per employee ended ~15% above pre-pandemic levels ([NBER w33555](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w33555/w33555.pdf)). MEASURED.
- **Per-labor-hour productivity** is tracked as transactions/covers/sales per labor hour (e.g., a worked example of 240 transactions / 20 labor-hours = 12 TPLH) ([Service Physics](https://www.servicephysics.com/operations-consulting-blog/what-is-tplh-transactions-per-labor-hour-the-key-metric-for-service-productivity/)). CLAIM (vendor benchmark). These are the units against which the physical action budget above is really spent.

One sentence relating this to the software dimension: because a single milk drink is ~20 physical motions and its KDS ticket is ~2-4 taps, the screen is a small fraction of the worker's action budget, so the POS/KDS design imperative is to add no avoidable tap on top of an already motion-heavy job — and, where possible, to help remove physical motion (routing, sequencing, mise en place), which is where the chains actually win.

## 4. Software actions per transaction (screen taps / clicks / steps)

### 4.1 There is no published "taps per order" statistic

No POS vendor and no peer-reviewed paper publishes a canonical count of taps to build and pay for an order. The count depends on the product, the item, and the number of modifiers. It must be derived. This report derives it two ways and labels both as ESTIMATE.

### 4.2 Derived from the vendor's own flow (Square, as the documented example)

Square's help documentation states the flow in words: "Tap an item tile on your item grid to add it to the cart," then "Tap **Charge**"; for a customized item, "Choose a variation... Select required and optional modifiers... tap **Add to cart**" ([build cart](https://squareup.com/help/us/en/article/8238-build-your-customer-s-cart-in-the-square-retail-pos-app)). The post-charge sequence is described as "cart → tip screen → card tap → done" ([tipping/flow](https://squareup.com/help/us/en/article/8631-set-up-and-customize-tipping)).

Counting the steps in that quoted flow (this count is the ESTIMATE; the flow text is Square's):

- Bare one-item cash/card sale: ~3 taps (item tile → Charge → confirm/tender).
- Each customized line item: +~3 taps (choose variation → select modifier(s) → Add to cart).
- Tip prompt when enabled: +1 tap.

So a simple 2-item coffee order with one modifier each lands near ~9-12 taps on Square's flow, and rises with every extra modifier. **Modifiers are the single largest inflator of tap-count.** This matches the general HCI finding that "extra taps, screens and steps increase the risk of abandoned carts" in mobile food ordering ([Paytronix](https://www.paytronix.com/blog/ordering-mobile-app), secondary/vendor).

### 4.3 Derived from the Keystroke-Level Model (HCI)

The KLM (Card, Moran & Newell, 1980; refined by Kieras, 1997) predicts expert, error-free task time by summing operator times ([Kieras](https://www.cs.umd.edu/~golbeck/INST631/KSM.pdf), [CACM original](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/358886.358895), [KLM-GOMS reference](https://www.usabilitybok.org/klm-goms/)):

- K (keystroke / button press): 0.20 s (0.12 s for a skilled typist).
- P (point to a target): 1.10 s.
- B (mouse press/release): 0.10 s.
- H (home hands between devices): 0.40 s.
- M (mental preparation / routine thinking): 1.35 s.
- R/W (system response): varies.

On a direct-touch POS there is no separate P operator for most taps (the finger goes straight to the target), so each deliberate tap costs roughly one K plus, at each real decision, one M. A ~15-tap order with, say, 3-4 genuine decision points is on the order of 15 × 0.2 s + 4 × 1.35 s ≈ 8-9 s of pure operator time for an expert cashier — before any customer conversation, reading, or system waits. This is a rough KLM ESTIMATE, not a measurement; the model's own average prediction error is ~20-21% ([Kieras](https://www.cs.umd.edu/~golbeck/INST631/KSM.pdf)). The load-bearing point is directional and reliable: **each avoidable tap and each avoidable decision point adds seconds that repeat on every transaction.**

### 4.4 Kiosk and mobile order flows

- One peer-reviewed field experiment at a Burger King (Torres, 2019, UNT) found kiosk ordering was faster than cashier ordering but yielded _lower_ sales per customer ([UNT](https://digital.library.unt.edu/ark:/67531/metadc1505242/)). MEASURED. The exact seconds and dollar figures are not in the publicly indexed record.
- Popular "kiosks cut order time ~40%" and "kiosks lift the average check ~20-30%" claims trace to vendor material (Tillster, Appetize, TouchBistro), not to a first-party or peer-reviewed owner ([TouchBistro blog, which itself cites others](https://www.touchbistro.com/blog/why-self-ordering-kiosks-are-becoming-the-secret-weapon-for-successful-restaurants/)). Treat as ESTIMATE/secondary; note that the one controlled study above points the _other_ way on sales.

## 5. Throughput per hour and per shift

### 5.1 Counter / coffee service is stated in service time, not transactions/hour

The chains that own this data publish a time-to-handoff, not a cashier transactions-per-hour figure:

- **Starbucks: ≤4-minute handoff target; ~50% of transactions already met it** on the CEO's first earnings call (Q4 FY2024) ([report](https://www.euronews.com/2024/10/31/starbucks-ceo-sets-four-minute-service-goal-to-reinvigorate-sales)). CLAIM. Starbucks discloses transaction _volume_ only as comparable-transaction change (e.g., US comparable transactions −4% in Q3 FY2025; US company-operated transactions +4.2% in Q3 FY2026) — not an absolute per-cashier rate ([Q3 FY2025 release](https://investor.starbucks.com/news/financial-releases/news-details/2025/Starbucks-Reports-Q3-Fiscal-Year-2025-Results/default.aspx), [Q3 FY2026 transcript](https://www.fool.com/earnings/call-transcripts/2026/08/07/starbucks-sbux-q3-2026-earnings-call-transcript/)). **Absolute peak transactions-per-half-hour: not found in a primary source** — Starbucks describes it only qualitatively.

### 5.2 Queueing/operations research gives measured service rates (lower-tier venues)

Academic case studies that time fast-food counters directly report a per-server service rate near ~40 customers/hour and a peak service time near ~3 minutes/customer, with total peak wait ~5.4 minutes (queue + service) ([M/M/c case study](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/387122148_Analysis_of_Queueing_Systems_in_Fast_Food_Restaurants_Using_the_MMc_Model_A_Case_Study_during_Peak_Hours)). MEASURED, but these are single-site studies in lower-tier operations journals; treat the exact numbers as indicative, not authoritative. Grocery-cashier benchmarks (a different task — scanning, not order building) sit around 5 minutes/transaction ≈ ~12 transactions/hour and ~9-22 items scanned per minute; these come from retail vendor material and are secondary ([Logile](https://www.logile.com/resources/blog/keeping-watch-key-metrics-in-measuring-cashier-performance)).

### 5.3 Drive-thru is the only channel with hard, measured, third-party throughput

The annual QSR Drive-Thru Study (data owned by Intouch Insight, formerly SeeLevel HX; published by QSR Magazine) uses undercover visits, so its seconds are MEASURED:

- **2025:** classic-segment average total time 5 min 9 s; average service time 4 min 15 s (3 min 53 s for AI-enabled ordering); order accuracy 87% ([Intouch 2025](https://www.intouchinsight.com/press-releases/drive-thru-study-2025-press-release)).
- **2024:** average total time ~5 min 29 s; accuracy 89% ([QSR 2024](https://www.qsrmagazine.com/story/the-2024-qsr-drive-thru-report/)).
- **2021 (SeeLevel HX):** average total time 382.39 s; an inaccurate order took +71.06 s longer, and an order-confirmation board cut ~34 s ([PR Newswire](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/seelevel-hx-21st-annual-drive-thru-study-uncovers-delays-and-inaccuracy-as-qsrs-struggle-with-labor-shortage-301383881.html)).

The +71 s penalty for an inaccurate order is the strongest single data point linking **accuracy to throughput**: errors are not just a quality cost, they directly consume line time.

### 5.4 Peak vs average

Every primary source frames capacity around the peak, not the average: Starbucks' whole Siren Craft/Green Apron program targets "busy moments" ([CX Dive](https://www.customerexperiencedive.com/news/starbucks-order-fulfillment-speedy-service-siren-craft-system/720896/)); Chipotle reports throughput as entrees in the _peak_ 15 minutes ("Max 15") ([Q2 2026](https://www.fool.com/earnings/call-transcripts/2026/08/07/chipotle-cmg-q2-2026-earnings-call-transcript/)). A specialty-coffee bar doing ~100-250 tickets/shift over ~8 hours averages ~12-31 tickets/hour, but the design constraint is the lunch/morning peak, where the rate is several times the average.

## 6. KDS interactions (software: taps to advance a ticket)

### 6.1 State lifecycle and per-ticket action count (from first-party docs)

Shipping KDS products converge on a short state machine and a low per-ticket action count. Counts below are ESTIMATES derived from the quoted flows:

- **Square KDS:** states are Open → Completed, color-coded in-progress/ready. To complete a whole ticket: "Tap the top banner of the ticket" — **1 tap**; a single item is one tap on that item. There is a **3-second undo**, and Recall is a tap in the completed view ([Square KDS](https://squareup.com/help/us/en/article/8171-complete-orders-with-square-kds)).
- **Toast KDS:** states are Preview → Sent → Fulfilled (+ Recalled). Touchscreen default is "**Double tap to fulfill**"; the bump bar has FULFILL, RECALL LAST, and VIEW buttons, and a whole ticket is cleared by pressing its numbered button "**twice in quick succession**" — so **~2 presses** ([Toast KDS overview](https://doc.toasttab.com/doc/platformguide/platformKDSOverview.html), [Toast bump bars](https://doc.toasttab.com/doc/platformguide/platformKitchenUsingBumpBars.html)).
- **Lightspeed KDS 2.0:** states are New (gray) → Preparing (blue) → Ready to collect (green) → Completed, plus On hold (brown) and Canceled (red). Advance with a **double-tap** per state, or per-item play → bell → checkmark buttons; **Undo** is available for **5 seconds** ([Lightspeed KDS 2.0](https://k-series-support.lightspeedhq.com/hc/en-us/articles/22708154090267-Using-the-Kitchen-Display-System-2-0)).
- **Fresh KDS:** a hardware bump bar lets staff "Scroll through orders, bump completed tickets, or recall past orders using button presses"; the exact per-ticket count is not documented ([Fresh KDS](https://www.fresh.technology/kds-features/bump-bar-support)).

Takeaway: the industry norm is **1-2 actions to advance or clear a ticket**, plus a short undo window, plus a recall path. A multi-state lifecycle (start → prepare → ready → bump) costs one action per transition only if each transition is a single, direct tap.

### 6.2 Tickets per station per shift, ticket times, routing

- **Order routing is documented, but no vendor publishes throughput numbers.** Square documents routing orders to a KDS by station/category; Lightspeed documents pulling DoorDash/Uber Eats/Grubhub into one kitchen queue. Neither states tickets-per-station-per-hour, ticket times, or all-day counts. **Tickets per station per shift: not found in a primary source.**
- **KDS effect on ticket time / errors: not found in a primary source.** The widely repeated "KDS cuts errors ~90% / cuts wait ~20-30% / boosts efficiency ~15-25%" figures appear only in secondary aggregator blogs (Lavu, others). No first-party vendor and no peer-reviewed study attaches a specific percentage. Treat all such KDS percentages as ESTIMATE/secondary and unverified.

## 7. Ergonomics and efficiency findings (HCI, human factors, queueing)

- **Fewer taps = less time, and the relationship is modelable.** The KLM predicts task time from operator counts within ~20-21% of observed time (Section 4.3). This is the core justification for minimizing taps on a high-repetition till ([Kieras](https://www.cs.umd.edu/~golbeck/INST631/KSM.pdf), [CACM](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/358886.358895)).
- **Fitts's law governs how fast a tap can be.** Target-acquisition time grows with distance to the target and shrinks with target size (Fitts, 1954, _J. Exp. Psychol._) ([APA record](https://psycnet.apa.org/record/1955-02059-001)). For a finger on a touchscreen, the Finger-Fitts (FFitts) model adds a finger-tremor/precision term, so small, tightly packed touch targets are disproportionately slower and more error-prone (Bi, Li & Zhai, CHI 2013) ([FFitts](https://www3.cs.stonybrook.edu/~xiaojun/pdf/FFitts.pdf)). Design consequence: large, well-spaced product and action tiles are faster _and_ reduce mis-taps — the two goals do not conflict.
- **Touch-target size has a normative floor.** WCAG 2.5.5 (Level AAA) requires targets of at least 44 × 44 CSS px ([W3C](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG21/Understanding/target-size.html)); Apple HIG recommends 44 pt and Material Design 48 dp ([Apple](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/accessibility), [Material](https://m3.material.io/foundations/designing/structure)). A POS/KDS used at speed, at arm's length, with wet or gloved hands, should sit at or above these floors, not below.
- **Errors cost throughput directly.** The drive-thru study's +71 s penalty for an inaccurate order (Section 5.3) quantifies what HCI predicts: a flow that prevents mis-entry (clear modifiers, confirmation of the built order) pays back in line time, not just customer satisfaction ([PR Newswire](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/seelevel-hx-21st-annual-drive-thru-study-uncovers-delays-and-inaccuracy-as-qsrs-struggle-with-labor-shortage-301383881.html)).
- **Queueing theory sets the target the UI must serve.** Peak service time near ~3 min/customer and a per-server rate near ~40/hour (Section 5.2) mean the till is the bottleneck at peak; shaving operator time per transaction raises the effective service rate and shortens the queue non-linearly near saturation ([M/M/c case study](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/387122148_Analysis_of_Queueing_Systems_in_Fast_Food_Restaurants_Using_the_MMc_Model_A_Case_Study_during_Peak_Hours)).

## 9. Relating to the Umi POS action model

This section compares the external benchmarks above to the established Umi baseline (given, not re-derived here). It flags where Umi looks competitive and where it looks heavy.

### 9.1 Cart building — competitive

- **Umi:** ~3-5 taps per line (product → modifier → add).
- **Benchmark:** Square's documented flow is ~3 taps per customized line (variation → modifier → Add to cart), and modifiers are the main inflator on both ([Square](https://squareup.com/help/us/en/article/8238-build-your-customer-s-cart-in-the-square-retail-pos-app)).
- **Verdict:** in line with the industry. Umi's per-line cost sits at or just above Square's; the top of Umi's range (5 taps) is worth checking for a removable step (e.g., a default variation that skips a screen for the common case).

### 9.2 Checkout / charge — heavy

- **Umi:** ~6 taps to charge (Cobrar → cash/exact amount → Revisar totales → Confirmar y cobrar → confirm dialog → Finalizar).
- **Benchmark:** Square's post-cart path is ~3 taps ("cart → tip screen → card tap → done") and payment auth is ~2 seconds ([Square flow](https://squareup.com/help/us/en/article/8631-set-up-and-customize-tipping), [Square press](https://squareup.com/us/en/press/square-makes-payments-even-faster)).
- **Verdict:** **Umi's charge path is the heaviest part of the flow relative to the benchmark** — roughly double Square's post-cart tap count. "Revisar totales," a "Confirmar y cobrar" step, _and_ a separate confirm dialog, _and_ "Finalizar" stack three confirmation-type actions where the benchmark has about one. By the KLM (Section 4.3), each removed tap/decision is real recurring time on a high-repetition action. **Recommendation to evaluate:** collapse "Revisar totales / Confirmar y cobrar / confirm dialog" into a single review-and-confirm for the exact-cash common case, keeping the extra confirmation only for edge cases (split, large change, refund). This is the clearest tap-count win.

### 9.3 KDS ticket advance — slightly heavy

- **Umi:** ~2-4 taps to advance a ticket; hands-on KDS ops ~6 (prepare, ready, complete, recall, priority, cancel-ack).
- **Benchmark:** Square = 1 tap to bump; Toast = double-tap / 2 presses; Lightspeed = double-tap per state, each with a short undo instead of a hard confirm ([Square KDS](https://squareup.com/help/us/en/article/8171-complete-orders-with-square-kds), [Toast KDS](https://doc.toasttab.com/doc/platformguide/platformKDSOverview.html), [Lightspeed KDS](https://k-series-support.lightspeedhq.com/hc/en-us/articles/22708154090267-Using-the-Kitchen-Display-System-2-0)).
- **Verdict:** the top of Umi's range (4 taps/advance) is heavier than every documented competitor. A specialty bar at 100-250 tickets/shift, each advanced through several states, makes this a high-frequency action. **Recommendation to evaluate:** make the most common transition (prepare→ready or ready→complete) a **single direct tap** with a 3-5 second undo (the Square/Lightspeed pattern), rather than a tap plus a confirm. Reserve multi-tap only for destructive actions (cancel). Umi's 6-op hands-on KDS catalog is itself reasonable and matches the state-machine richness of Lightspeed (which also exposes On hold, Cancel, priority-like ordering).

### 9.4 Shift operations — reasonable, because they are one-time

- **Umi:** open-shift ~5 actions once; close-shift ~7-9 actions once (count → resolve → reconcile → close).
- **Benchmark:** no vendor publishes a shift open/close tap-count. These are once-per-shift, not per-transaction, so their tap-count barely affects throughput.
- **Verdict:** not a throughput concern. The 48 permissioned operations / ~23 everyday operations for the cashier role is a permission-surface question, not a per-transaction speed question; it does not inflate the hot path.

### 9.5 Throughput target Umi should design for

- Umi's ~100-250 tickets/shift ≈ ~12-31 tickets/hour average, several times that at the morning/lunch peak. The relevant external target is Starbucks' **≤4-minute order-to-handoff at peak** ([report](https://www.euronews.com/2024/10/31/starbucks-ceo-sets-four-minute-service-goal-to-reinvigorate-sales)) — a coffee-specific, first-party number — and the measured fast-food peak service time near ~3 min/customer ([case study](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/387122148_Analysis_of_Queueing_Systems_in_Fast_Food_Restaurants_Using_the_MMc_Model_A_Case_Study_during_Peak_Hours)). The register and KDS taps are a small but repeated fraction of that budget; the larger levers, per the big-company evidence, are **order sequencing, station routing, and accuracy** (Starbucks' −2-minute sequencing algorithm; Chipotle's second make line; the drive-thru study's +71 s inaccuracy penalty), not raw tap-count alone. Umi's tap-count is competitive at the cart and heavy at the two confirm-stacked points (checkout and KDS advance); fixing those, and investing in sequencing/routing/accuracy, matches what the leaders actually did.

### 9.6 Where the evidence is weak

- No primary source gives a canonical "taps per order" or "taps per ticket"; every such count here (Umi's and the vendors') is a step-count ESTIMATE, not a measurement.
- KDS error/time-reduction percentages and kiosk check-uplift percentages have no first-party or peer-reviewed owner; they are excluded from the comparison except as flagged secondary claims.
- Starbucks' most recent Mobile Order % and any absolute per-cashier transactions/hour are not found in a primary source; the coffee-shop throughput target is expressed as a time-to-handoff, which is what Umi should adopt as its own metric.

## Surprises worth noting

- The industry does **not** publish tap-counts. The most defensible external anchor is an HCI model (KLM) from 1980, not a vendor benchmark. Umi's own instrumented tap-counts are therefore more precise than anything public — an advantage, if Umi measures real cashier sessions.
- The one controlled kiosk study (Torres, 2019) contradicts the ubiquitous "+20-30% check" marketing claim: kiosks were faster but sold _less_ per customer ([UNT](https://digital.library.unt.edu/ark:/67531/metadc1505242/)). The uplift numbers should not be trusted without a primary source.
- The largest measured throughput lever is **accuracy**, not speed: an inaccurate drive-thru order cost +71 s ([SeeLevel 2021](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/seelevel-hx-21st-annual-drive-thru-study-uncovers-delays-and-inaccuracy-as-qsrs-struggle-with-labor-shortage-301383881.html)). A confirm step that prevents a wrong order can be worth more than the tap it costs — which is the counter-argument to over-trimming Umi's checkout confirmations. Trim the redundant confirmations; keep the one that verifies the order.
- Every big chain's throughput win came from **sequencing, routing, and role design** (Starbucks Siren Craft/Green Apron, McDonald's Ready on Arrival, Chipotle's second make line), not from a faster register UI. Tap-count is necessary hygiene; it is not where the leaders found their minutes.
- **The physical action budget is an order of magnitude larger than the software one.** A milk drink is ~20 bodily motions (derived) vs ~2-4 KDS taps; a QSR ticket is ~10-20 hand actions vs 1-2 bump taps. And the physical side is measurably harmful — tamping alone can exceed the NIOSH 3,400 N spine-compression limit, and a busy bar runs the hands past the >10 reps/min upper-limb injury threshold ([Dainty 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24837283/), [Texas DWC](https://www.tdi.texas.gov/pubs/videoresource/fsergofood.pdf)). So the POS/KDS should never add a motion the worker's body then pays for; the software's best contribution is to remove physical motion (routing, sequencing, mise en place), the lever the chains actually pulled.
