## 1. Method and confidence

### 1.1 Method

- I read first-party sources only: vendor sites, vendor help centers, vendor developer
  documentation, and vendor price pages.
- I used `curl` for static pages. I used the `r.jina.ai` text renderer for JavaScript pages
  that `curl` returns as an empty shell. The rendered content is the official page. The two
  JavaScript surfaces are `clover.com/help` and the Fudo help center.
- Every claim in the matrix carries a source key in brackets, for example `(FU3)`. Section 7
  maps each key to one URL.
- I found features for the five vendors in the prior art. I re-read the prior-art notes and
  added only new facts. The prior-art notes themselves are sources `PA1`, `PA2` and `PA3`.
- I could not verify a fact when the official pages do not state it. I write `unknown` in the
  matrix and I list the item in section 6.

### 1.2 Sources read in this pass

**Fudo** — the real domains are `fu.do`, `app.fu.do`, `soporte.fu.do`, `recursos.fu.do` and
`dev.fu.do`. The domains in the brief (`fudo.co`, `ayuda.fudo.co`, `app.fudo.co`) are not
Fudo's. `fudo.co` is a parked domain (HTTP 403). I read the full help-center sitemap
(611 URLs), the 20 marketing and legal pages, the 172-entry official changelog, and the
Mexico price page. Confidence: **high**.

**SoftRestaurant** — I read `softrestaurant.com` (home, Soft Restaurant 12, price,
requirements, all six add-on pages, cloud, payments terminal, hardware catalog) and the
Zoho Desk knowledge base index and categories. Confidence: **medium**. The public knowledge
base documents installation, invoicing, printers, and add-ons. It does not publish the user
manual or the role model. I mark those rows `unknown`.

**Clover** — I read the Clover help center (`clover.com/en-US/help/...`), the Kitchen Display
System page, the restaurant page, the employee-management page, and the restaurant price
page. Clover's help center is a JavaScript application, so I read it through the text
renderer. Confidence: **medium-high**. Clover's public articles are short. Several older
article URLs are now dead, and I dropped them.

**Square, Toast, Odoo, Lightspeed, PoloTab** — I read new first-party pages for roles,
tables, seats, coursing, inventory, recipes, reporting, franchises, pricing, and permissions.
Confidence: **high** for Toast, Odoo and Lightspeed, **medium-high** for Square and PoloTab.

### 1.3 Confidence rules used in the matrix

- `yes` = an official page states the capability, with an example or a step list.
- `partial` = an official page states only part of the capability, or the capability needs a
  paid module, a higher plan, or a third-party app.
- `no` = the official corpus does not state the capability. Search terms for the Fudo `no`
  rows are listed at the end of section 6.
- `unknown` = the official pages are silent and I could not test the product.

## 3. Per-vendor profiles

### 3.1 Fudo

Fudo sells one system for the whole restaurant in Spanish-speaking Latin America. The vendor
claims "más de 30.000 negocios" and names Mexico, Argentina, Chile, Colombia, Peru, Paraguay
and Uruguay, with a separate Brazil brand (FU1, FU2). The target is wide: independent
venues and small chains, from cafes to dark kitchens (FU2). The pricing model is a cheap
core plus modules. Mexico lists Plan Inicial at $360 MXN per month, Plan Avanzado at $690 and
Plan Pro at $1,050, without tax (FU1). Tables, per-diner sales, KDS, delivery apps, electronic
invoicing and the AI receptionist are separate monthly modules; Recepcionista IA costs $850,
more than the Pro plan itself (FU1). The product is cloud-only. It states that an internet
connection is indispensable, and it publishes no local server (FU24). The strongest idea is
the module ladder on a cheap, complete core. A venue can start with counter sales, cash and
a QR menu, then buy capability as it grows. The second strong idea is channel unification.
One menu, one price list and one stock pool feed the counter, QR self-order, WhatsApp orders
and every aggregator (FU21, FU22). Fudo is the closest vendor to Umi in language and
culture. Its weak points are the missing loyalty program, the missing gift cards, the missing
offline mode and the thin role presets.

### 3.2 SoftRestaurant

SoftRestaurant is the incumbent in Mexico. It is made by National Soft, claims "Más de 42.000
restaurantes en 14 países" and sells software that is more than 25 years old (SR1). Two
products run side by side. Soft Restaurant 12 is a Windows desktop system with a local server
and workstations. Soft Restaurant Cloud is a cloud POS with a web administrator and an
Android app (SR3, SR17). The desktop system costs $799 MXN per month for LITE and $1,099 for
PRO; the cloud costs $699, $999 or $1,599 for the multi-branch plan, all plus tax (SR2, SR3).
The target is the full range of Mexican food businesses: restaurants, cafes, fast food,
bars, pizzerias, taquerias and dark kitchens (SR1). The strongest idea is the security
profile system that guards money. The owner builds a profile instead of choosing a fixed
role, marks each permission, and can require an authorized password or a fingerprint for
each sensitive action (SR17, SR16). The guarded actions are cancellations, discounts,
comps, re-open, reprints and price changes. The system records the event and can send a
WhatsApp or email alert (SR26). That design answers the main fear of a restaurant owner.
Its weak points are a Windows-first desktop stack, thin public documentation, no gift-card
API, no AI, and old-fashioned hardware.

### 3.3 Clover

Clover is a Fiserv company. It sells a closed loop of hardware, software and processing
through merchant banks and resellers. The restaurant plans are Register, Table Service
Restaurant and Counter Service Restaurant (CL8). Software prices are not published; the
buyer gets a quote. Hardware prices are published, and the Kitchen Display System costs $899
for the 24-inch model and $799 for the 14-inch model, plus $25 per device per month (CL8).
The target is small and medium food businesses in the United States, Canada, Ireland and
Singapore (CL16). The strongest idea is the dining module as a first-class product. Clover
Dining opens tables, assigns items to guests, changes courses, splits checks, transfers
tables and takes payment from the same device (CL5). Auto-coursing fires courses on a
schedule, and the operator can adjust each firing time during service (CL7). The second
strong idea is the KDS bundle: a heat-resistant screen, a bump bar accessory, item-level
routing, expo mode, prep-time reporting and runner-ticket printing to any network printer
(CL8). Clover's weak points are thin public documentation, a public help center that hides
detail behind a JavaScript application, and a merchant-bank sales model that makes the total
price hard to learn.

### 3.4 Square for Restaurants

Square sells one cloud graph across POS, online, kiosk, KDS and delivery. Plans are Free,
Plus at $49 and Premium at $149 per location per month (PA3). KDS and Kiosk are per-device
add-ons (PA3). The target is the whole small-business market, with a restaurant
configuration inside it. Square's strongest idea is seat-level service. Square tracks a
cover count and a seat position per guest, sorts the cart by seat, prints kitchen tickets
sorted by seat, and attaches allergen and dietary notes to a seat (SQ5). Coursing then
groups items into named courses that hold and fire (SQ6). The second strong idea is the
channel menu: one menu controls the POS, online ordering, kiosks and delivery apps, with
time-based availability and per-location prices (SQ26). Square also publishes location price
overrides (SQ24) and a franchise tier with menu concepts and franchise reports (SQ22, SQ23).
Its weak points for a cafe are full-service bias in the table features, a beta recipe
feature that cannot handle nested recipes or modifiers, and an iPad POS with an
Android-only KDS (SQ9, SQ27).

### 3.5 Toast

Toast is a full restaurant operating system. It sells proprietary hardware, POS, processing
and a SaaS suite on one back office. Plans are Starter at $0 with higher processing, Point of
Sale at $69, and a custom build (PA3). Money comes from processing, add-ons, hardware and
lending (PA3). The target is restaurants of every size in the United States, Canada, the
United Kingdom and Ireland (PA3). The strongest idea for Umi is the permission model. Toast
does not sell roles. It publishes about 100 named access permissions in nine groups, from POS
modes and cash drawers to reporting, setup and account administration, and the owner bundles
them into jobs (TO1). Every permission states what it grants and whom to assign it to. That
is the clearest role documentation in the market. Toast's second strong idea is the KDS:
assembly lines, load balancing, partial fulfilment, a runner mode and on-screen recipes
(PA2). Its weak points are cost pushed into processing rates, soft loyalty and CRM depth,
and pricing that needs a quote.

### 3.6 Odoo POS and Odoo Restaurant

Odoo is an open-source ERP of about 50 applications that share one database. The POS is one
module, and Community is free. Enterprise adds Studio, advanced reporting and hosting
options, and Enterprise is priced per user (OD9). The target is companies that want one
system for sales, stock, accounting and HR, not only a cash register. Odoo's strongest idea
is the documented three-level employee rights model. Odoo names Minimal, Basic and Advanced
POS rights and lists the exact action for each level, from lock and unlock register to cancel
orders and change prices, plus a PIN or a badge for login (OD1). That is a superb template
for a role design. Its second strong idea is the Preparation Display, which routes by POS and
product category, uses named stages with colors, and sets an alert timer per stage (OD2).
Odoo also exposes QR menu, QR ordering and kiosk from one setting (OD3). Its weak points for
a cafe are a generic hospitality interface, an ERP learning curve, and a browser-based
preparation display.

### 3.7 Lightspeed Restaurant (K-Series)

Lightspeed sells a modular cloud platform on iPad. The plans are Starter at $69, Essential at
$189 and Premium at $399 per month, and the KDS costs $30 per screen per month (PA3). The
target is restaurants, bars and multi-site groups, mostly in North America, Europe and
Australia. Lightspeed's strongest idea is its KDS 2.0. It routes by station, pulses when a
wait time passes a threshold, and mirrors the "Ready" state back to online-ordering partners
(LS7). Its second strong idea is documented role grouping. Lightspeed ships four POS user
groups — Floor staff, Manager, Trainee and Shift tracker — and adds separate Back Office
permissions, with per-user overrides (LS1, LS2). The Trainee group processes practice
transactions that do not touch sales data, and the Shift tracker group can only clock in and
out (LS2). That is a clean, small role model. Its weak points are payments and hardware
lock-in, hidden processing rates, an iPad-only stack, and loyalty that depends on Marsello
(LS9, LS11).

### 3.8 PoloTab

PoloTab is a Mexico-only POS for cafes and restaurants, sold as bundled hardware plus
software. It claims 900 venues and 22.8 million orders per year, and its wedge is 24/7
Spanish support (PT10, PT11). The plans are token plans — Esencial, Avanzado and Pro — and
hardware and processing are separate (PT10, PT11). The target is the same cafe and
restaurant market that Umi serves, which makes PoloTab the closest direct comparable. Its
strongest idea for Umi is the role screen in the admin portal: the owner creates a role and
toggles named permissions, and the vendor explicitly recommends restricting discounts,
cancellations, price changes and cash adjustments (PT1). Its second strong idea is the
recipe chain: recipes, sub-recipes and prepared items all feed inventory costing (PT4, PT5,
PT6, PT7). PoloTab also aggregates delivery apps into one screen and offers a custom report
builder (PT8, PT11). Its weak points are the missing loyalty program, the missing gift
cards, no own-brand web store and no self-order kiosk (PT11).

### 4.1 What the role models teach

- Toast is the reference for granularity. It documents about 100 permissions in nine groups.
  Every permission states what it grants and whom to assign it to. The owner bundles
  permissions into jobs (TO1).
- Lightspeed is the reference for simplicity. Four group types plus per-user overrides cover
  a whole venue (LS2). The Trainee group is a useful safety idea: practice sales never touch
  real data.
- SoftRestaurant is the reference for protecting money. The profile is free-form. A sensitive
  permission can demand a password or a fingerprint at the moment of use. The guarded list is
  small and precise: cancel, discount, comp, re-open and reprint (SR16).
- Odoo is the reference for plain language. Three named rights levels with an explicit action
  list are easy to explain to an owner (OD1).
- Square and Clover are the reference for identity. Square uses a personal passcode or a
  physical badge, and it scopes every team member to locations (SQ2, SQ3). Clover uses one
  passcode length per device and builds custom roles from three defaults (CL1, CL4).
- Fudo and PoloTab show the LatAm shape. Fudo ships three preset roles and a large permission
  catalogue, with a superuser that cannot be given a role (FU3, FU5). PoloTab keeps it small:
  create a role, toggle permissions, and restrict discounts, cancellations, price changes and
  cash adjustments (PT1).
- Market gaps. Only Toast, Square and Lightspeed separate a role from a permission cleanly.
  Fudo, Clover and SoftRestaurant mix the two. Tip entitlement is almost never a role
  property. Only Square and Toast name tip-out roles.

## 6. Gaps and opportunities for Umi

1. **Roles are a real differentiator, and the market is weak in the middle.** Toast has 100
   permissions and Odoo has three plain levels (TO1, OD1). Fudo, Clover and SoftRestaurant
   mix roles and permissions (FU3, CL1, SR17). Umi can win with a small, named role set
   (owner, manager, supervisor, cashier, waiter, kitchen, host, viewer) plus a per-permission
   override list. Evidence: this is exactly what Toast and Odoo do, and what PoloTab's help
   center tells owners to configure (PT1).
2. **Per-role table authorization and approval-on-action is a LatAm pattern with no
   competitor equivalent in English markets.** Fudo blocks a waiter from touching another
   waiter's table with a PIN (FU31). SoftRestaurant requires an authorized password or a
   fingerprint for cancel, discount, comp, re-open and reprint (SR16). Umi can make
   manager approval a first-class, role-driven flow on the POS and the KDS.
3. **Offline is still an open field.** Fudo states that the internet is indispensable (FU24).
   Square allows offline card payments only inside a time window and accepts the risk of a
   decline later (SQ21). Toast needs a Local Hub (PA2). SoftRestaurant is the only vendor
   that documents desktop offline plus queue-and-sync plus a local network (SR1, SR17). Umi
   already has a device identity and a durable journal. Offline-complete is the strongest
   claim Umi can make.
4. **Loyalty, gift cards and WhatsApp are a LatAm gap.** Fudo has no loyalty points, no gift
   cards and no stored value (FU2). PoloTab has none either (PT11). Square and Toast own
   these features in the United States (SQ17, PA2). Umi already has a points ledger, gift
   cards and WhatsApp identity. Ship tiers and a reward catalogue and Umi leads the region.
5. **Recipes are the deepest moat in the market.** SoftRestaurant documents sub-recipes,
   unit conversion and cooking loss (SR18). PoloTab documents recipes, prepared items and
   sub-recipes (PT4, PT5, PT6). Square admits that its own recipe feature cannot nest
   recipes, cannot put recipes on modifiers and cannot attach a recipe to a multi-variation
   item (SQ9). Umi can take the top of this module with nested recipes and modifier
   consumption.
6. **Table timers and turn time are simple and rare.** Clover turns a table red after 90
   minutes by default (CL6). Square turns a table yellow and then red at set times (SQ4).
   Fudo has no turn-time metric at all (FU26). A per-role table timer plus a turn-time
   report is cheap and visible.
7. **KDS staging is the clearest product gap for Umi.** Fudo has no course concept and no
   recall (FU11). SoftRestaurant has a two-hour recall with an "R" marker and window and
   linear views (SR21, SR23). Clover has expo mode and multi-level fulfilment (CL8). Umi's
   KDS client is read-only today, so per-item state, recall and staging are the highest
   value build.
8. **Pricing transparency is a trust weapon.** Clover hides software prices (CL8, CL17).
   Umi can publish every price, as Fudo, SoftRestaurant, Square, Odoo, Lightspeed and PoloTab
   do (FU1, SR2, PA3, OD9, LS12, PT10).
